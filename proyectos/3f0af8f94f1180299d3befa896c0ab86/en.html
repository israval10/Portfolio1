▪ Why Design Thinking + CRO

Most web redesigns fail because they treat the problem as esthetic When it's structural. Apply Design Thinking to a CRO project requires you to ask the right questions in the right order: first understand the user and his actual pain, then define the problem with precision and only then design - with every visual decision anchored to a measurable conversion hypothesis.

That case studied followed the five canonical phases - To empathize, Define, Ideate, Protoctipar and Test - and close with a reflection phase that document the apprenticeships for future projects.

FASE 1 - AMPATIZAR

To understand the business and the team and final client before questioning anything.

1.1 The starting point

LlamaLeads is a growing marketing agency that already had a business functioning. They generated interbound traffic through paid campaigns and organic search and a four-person team with proven track record delivering results to SMB customers in Peru and Colombia. The problem was not service - it was window.

His web was seen as any other agency: blue gradients, isometric stock illustrations, a grid of 6 services, and a form buried at the end of a long scroll. Visitors came curious and went unconverted, not because LlamaLeads was the right choice but because The place never gave them a reason to believe it was..

The initial briefing was direct: Redesign the web to turn traffic into trained leads. But the underlying challenge was strategic - LlamaLeads needed to stop communicating What do they do? and start communicating Why does it matter?.

FASE 2 - DEFINITION

To put research into a precise and enforceable problem.

2.1 Status of the problem

The LlamaLeads site indicates what the agency does but doesn't matter why. The visitor arrives with a specific business pain and finds a catalogue of services - generating a mismatch between the buyer's intention and the contents of the page that systematically kills conversion.

That's no aesthetic problem. It's a problem of sequencing: The site starts talking about LlamaLeads - who they are, what they offer, how they work - before they have given a single reason to care about them. To sell high-thought B2B services, that order's exactly the opposite.

2.3 How could we... (How Might We)

I Reformulated challenges as design opportunities:

  • How could we? to open the page with such a precise problem That the ideal client felt that was written for him?
  • How could we? compress visual system so that each item indicates authority rather than familiarity?
  • How could we? make the delivery process so tangible That a prospect feels secure and engaging before a call date?
  • How could we? answer top 3 objections from the buyer before they are made, inside his own page?
  • How could we? place social proof at the exact moment of best doubt, isn't it at the end of scroll?

Convert the problem into an architecture strategy, visual system and CRO.

3.3 CRO Principles applying to each Section

  • Oran-the- fold clarity - the hero communicates the central outcome in less than 5 seconds (validated with 5-second test)
  • One Primary TTA per Section - with no competitive options and zero decision paralysis
  • Progressive commitment - 3 micro- Tens distributed throughout scroll heat up the buyer before the main form
  • Elimination of objections before conversion - FAQ and comparative put at 75-85% scroll, not at the end
  • Social proof at the moment of best doubt - equipment just before the closing CTA, not at the foot
  • Friction audit - reduced form from 7 to 4 fields (deleted: URL, budget, message)

4.3 Three decisions that have changed everything

Decision 1 - Rewrite of the Hero

Before"Digital Marketing Agency"
Later"We design the growing system you need"
Why does that work?It goes from category label (that anyone can demand) to specific outcome promise (that they only fulfill). It changes the emotional record of the whole page from "this is what we do" to "this is what you get."

Decision 2 - The problem statement as an autonomous section

"The problem isn't to generate more leads. It's not to have a system that will turn them."

- Problem statement Section, scroll depth 20%

That line does three things at once: 1. Name the problem that the buyer has been unable to articulate 2. Reformulate the "best lead" solution to "best conversion" 3. He points out that LlamaLeads understands the actual issue - what's a sign of confidence before we mention any service

The Insight about the Origin of this sentence: came about textual from an objection that the foot had repeatedly heard at sales calls. A copywriter didn't write it. excavated from a conversation. The designer's work often isn't to create the message but to recognize it when it appears and put it in the right place.

Decision 3 - To move the equipment before the FTA and after

On most agency websites, the computer is an afterthought: a row of photos at the end. In redesign, the equipment section is positioned immediately before the closing CTA.

The reasoning is psychological: the moment of greatest doubt in a B2B conversion is The instant before the form. To place real faces with names and roles exactly at that time turns the abstract agency language into a human commitment.

The data back them up: in post-launch session recordings, users arriving at the equipment section had a complete form rate 3 × greater That's what they did earlier.

REFLEXION - Learning and following steps

) What I learned

Strategy before pixels isn't a cliche - it's the margin

The most striking week of the project was the first. Heuristic eval, stakeholder interview and analytics audit took 6 days. The design took 3 weeks. But the design was only as good as the brief and the brief was only as good as research. Without the stage of discovery, redesign would have been a visual refresh rather than a structural fix.

The buyer's language's always better than yours.

The most powerful sentence of redesign - "The problem isn't to generate more leads. It's to have no system to turn them." - came about textual from an objection that the foot had repeatedly heard. It was not written by a copywriter. It was dug from a conversation. The designer's work often isn't to create the message but to recognize it when it appears and put it in the right place.

Positioning social proof's a sequence problem, not content.

Most agency websites have social proof. Almost none have him in the right place. The testimonies at the bottom of the page are irrelevant - the buyer who arrives there has already made a decision. The Team Section of LlamaLeads works because it appears at the moment that the buyer is closer to leaving. Filling the social proof correctly means more than adding more social proof.
