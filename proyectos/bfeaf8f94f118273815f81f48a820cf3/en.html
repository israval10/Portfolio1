EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Datapiers is an educational web platform that cataloges UX metrics and organizes them under Google's HEART framework. It's designed for product designers and students who need to make data-supported design decisions, in a context where information about UX metrics are dispersed, in English and disconnected from real cases.

The problem

The designer at LATM learns UX metric from fragments: Twitter threads, academic papers, blogs translated without context. When the time comes to decide what to measure against a real DIREL, knowledge does not translate into criterion. The result is a presentation to the client where the design decisions are held in intuition, and not evidence.

The solution

A platform with three entrance doors - Métrics, Framework and Rils - that allows users to get to the concept from where it's most natural. Each metric has a consistent tab that always answers the same questions: why it matters, how it's computed, where to apply it, what companies use and what tip UX holds its use. The HEART taxonomy works as a leading thread that binds the five dimensions of user behavior - Happiness, Engagement, Adoption, Retention, Task Success - with every metric of the catalogue.

The expected impact

  • Understanding: The user locates the metric they require in less than 60 seconds.
  • Trust: He puts his decisions with a replicable technical argument, but his decisions are unimprovised.
  • Community: consolidate a common vocabulary about Spanish-language metrics, anchored at HEART.

PROCESS - How I worked this project

I applied Design Thinking in five phases (empathize, Define, Ideate, Protoctipar, Test) and treated them as iterative cycles, not as linear steps. Each phase produced a dedicated concrete that fed the following, and the findings of the testing gave me back to empathy as what I learned required.

Figure 1. Overview of the Design Thinking process applied to datapipiers.

The criterion for returning to an earlier phase was always the same: if a design decision could not be supported by something that research had revealed, research was insufficient. That principle, which works as an operational guide to the project, became also the product's thesis: decisions are held in evidence, and not in preference.

_ Listen before designing

I started without assuming what a designer needs to make datat- information decisions. I wanted to understand the actual friction, not the one I thought. That's how I combined three methods: in-depth interviews, a quantitative survey and a competitive analysis of the sources that the target public already consumes.

Research methods

Semi-structured interviews (N = 6)

45 minutes with 3 midlevel designers at digital companies from Lima and Bogotá, two Juniors at agencies and a seventh cycle student at Graphic Design. The guide revolved around three questions: how do you decide what to measure when you get a briefing, how do you find information about a new metric and how do you propose your decisions to an PM or client?

Quantitative survey (N = 42)

Distributed in LinkedIn and Discord UX communities. I have been using hypotheses about frequency of using metrics, regular tools and level of safety while defending decisions. Three data confirmed the project direction:

  • 78% recognised the HEART framework by name but only 23% applied it structured.
  • 64% look for information about metrics at Google first, and not at formal courses.
  • 81% considers their level of insecurity to be "moderate" or "high" by translating UX findings into business metrics.

Competition analysis

I studied 12 sources that target public already consumes: Nielsen Norman Group, Smashing Magazine, Mobbin, UX Collective, Coursera, Coderhouse, as well as four Twitter threads and two newsletters. Most have quality content but none offer a consistent taxonomy that allows to compare metric with each other. That was the crack that Datapiers decided to occupy.

Empathy Map

Synthetic interviews at an Empathy Map forced me to separate what the user says from what the user does and feels. The distance between these four dimensions is where the Insight lives: Carolina says she can measure but thinks she lacks criterion and does A / B tests but she's insecure about defending results.

FASE INSIGHT

The user's knowledge isn't the problem. That's confidence by translating that knowledge into defensible arguments about stakeholders.

Persons

Research became two archetypes. I did not build them as decorative fictions: every demographic attribute is supported by public data about the Peruvian and Latin American digital ecosystem (DataReportal 2024, Glassdoor, AméricaEconomics, Vertiv). I put them next to each other because they share a central friction, but they live them at different intensities.

Figure 3. Comparative synthesis of both User Persons: Carolina (mid-level) and Mariana (student).

Carolina López - The designer who needs to defend what she already knows

29 years old, mid- level at a startup fintech light. He knows his tools, has been training at Coursera and Coderhouse and works with his first and first mobile flows with Figma and Amptitude. The friction for Carolina isn't to learn - it's to synthesize and argue. When an PM asks for "theoretical fundamentals" after his decision to move a button, the conversation becomes a re-translation effort that was not his initial brief.

Mariana Torres - The student who wants to apply what she's learning

21 years old, seventh cycle of graphic design. He attends UX / UI extracurricular workshops and hears about the state of art by TikTok and YouTube rather than by the curricular mesh. Her friction is opposite to that of Carolina: she has time and motivation to learn but lacks real projects where to apply Learning. He needs a resource that puts his place between academic theory and industry cases.

DIRECTORATE DECISION

A single platform for both profiles. The depth is chosen by the user while browsing: Mariana enters through the framework door to learn the theory and Carolina enters through the Métricas door to solve an individual case.

Customer Journey Map

I mapped the Carolina Journey at the most representative stage: a redesign brief where you have to defend a metric against your PM. I worked five phases - Discover, Investigate, Filter, Apply, Defend - and for each I documented actions, thoughts, emotions, pain points and opportunities. The emotional curve was the most revealing fact: frustration doesn't appear at first, it appears at the centre of the journey, just where the user has to filter information without a clear criterion.

FASE 02 · DEFINIR - Synthetic to find the actual problem

Research generated nearly 200 notes: interviewing, survey observations and competitive findings. The phase of definition was to put that material together, to find the dominant issues and to translate them into an enforceable problem.

Affinity Diagram

I worked clustering at FigJam, moving notes till four issues emerged and they supported each other. They weren't labels I chose. They were groups that the material formed with little intervention from me. When the notes were naturally grouped about "fragmentation of knowledge," "theory without applying," "distance with business" and "need for reference," I knew research had reached saturation.

Figure 5. Affinity Diagram. The four clusters converge into a single key insight.

Of the four clusters I distilled three POV statements in "User + Need + Insight" format. They work as a compass: Any feature that you design should be able to fight back with one of them.

POVStatement
POV-1Carolina, a midlevel designer, needs a clear taxonomy of UX metrics because information scattering forces her to improvise criteria every time she gets a brief.
POV-2Mariana, a design student, needs applied examples of metrics in real companies because academic theory doesn't give her concrete evidence with which to build his portfolio.
POV3Both people need a bridge between metric and business argument because without them their design decisions are perceived as subjective by their stakeholders.

I turned every POV into how Might We questions - the formulation that opens up the exploration space without tying up the solution. These are the ones that survived the filtering:

  • HMW-1 offer a visual taxonomy that will allow recognition of a metric in less than 10 seconds.
  • HMW-2 to connect each metric with actual company use cases, but with separate definitions.
  • HMW-3 To allow users to filter metric by performance dimension (what they want to measure), but not by name.
  • HMW-4 include an UX tip by metric that the designer can get to his next meeting.
The designers and students at LATM have no information about UX metrics and have a cured, visual and applicable map that will allow them to identify the right metric for a particular problem and to uphold their decision with evidence against stakeholders.

FASE 03 · IDEAR - From problem to backlog

With a firm problem statement, the idea stopped being an open brainstorming and became a succession of filters. Three tools guided the phase: Jobs To Be Do to ensure that every idea responded to a real job, User Stories to translate JTBD into implementation and a prioritization matrix to decide what entered the academic MVP.

Jobs To Be Do

The JTBD framework forced me to think about the product as a "metric web," but as a work that users hire. The most interesting thing came about by separating the three dimensions of the job: the functional one is just the tip of the iceberg and the actual weight is in the emotional and social dimension.

Figure 6. Canvas Jobs To Be Do with job statement and three dimensions of work.

PRODUCT REFIRE

Datapiers doesn't sell information about metrics. It sells professional confidence to the user who has to uphold a design decision with data. All information we offer is car, no end.

Priority matrix

With fourteen feature candidates on the table, I needed a criterion to decide which ones entered the academic MVP. I used an Impact vs. Effort matrix, where Impact was weighted according to POVs coverage (how many POVs solve each feature) and Effort according to estimated hours at Figma + editing time.

Figure 7. Matrix Impact vs. Efforts. The MVP range includes complete QuickWins and the most strategic Big Bets.

What entered MVP

The final range concentrates seven pieces: catalogue of metric with HEART filters (F1, F2), details sheet with rich template (F3), page dedicated to the HEART Framework (F5), cases of metric use (F4), tip UX (F7) and external resources (F6). It's the minimum combination that cover the three POVs and allows to test the central hypothesis of the product.

What's been put out (and why)

  • Page of Roles (F9): remains as a navigable skeleton with an "In Development" mark to validate with users an interest without building the logic of recommendation yet.
  • Stretch and account system (F10): The local storage cover the user's promise at 80% of the cost.
  • Community and comments (F11): high care and restraint are required and cannot be tested with an academic MVP.
  • Multilingual EN / EN (F14): The proposal differs by talking to LATM in Spanish and the English dilute the bet.
PRINCIPLE OF PRIORIZATION

Better five features than the user loves, than twelve that fulfill the list. The matrix was a tool to say no, not to say yes.

FASE 04 · PROTOTIpAR

Building the experience

The prototipate took three weeks and was resolved on four parallel fronts: information architecture, design system, editorial content and high-fidelity screen composition. Each front fed the next with pieces that were already tested.

Information architecture

The first deliverable was a sitemap that responded to a simple question: what are the user's mental routes? Research had revealed three forms of entry: the one that looks for a concrete metric (Carolina with his brief), the one that wants to learn complete theory (Mariana in exploration) and the one that comes from a specific role (a curious Product Manager). The IA reflects these three forms as first level branches: Metrics, Framework and Rils.

Figure 8. Information architecture. A single taxonomy (HEART) crosses all four branches.

ARQUITECANIC DECISION

A single taxonomy - HEART - crosses all three branches. Taxonomic consistency is what allows the user to leap between Framework and Metrics without reconfiguring his mental model.

Service Bluefrint

The blueprint extends the journey to the invisible processes that sustain the experience. I built it because, while Datapiors isn't a service in the classical sense, it does depend on a constant editorial work that the user doesn't see: caseshow, resource update, visual system maintenance. Documentalizing this makes clear what remains within the Academic MVP and what the Roadmap of the next phase requires.

Figure 9. Service Blueprint with the five moments of use and the support processes that enable them.

Design system

The visual system came from a restriction that became virtue: HEART has five dimensions, so the system needs five functional colors before anything else. Each dimension has a color and an icon that are consistently repeated in cards, filters, illustrations and detailed blocks. When the user sees a yellow circle with a trophy, he knows that he's looking at some Task Success - before reading his label.

HEART dimensionColorMeaning in system

RetentionPurpleProduct capacity to retain the user in time.

On this five-color basis, the system adds typography (Arial for long text, a hypographic display for hero and sections), a 12-column grid, isometric black and white illustrations with color hales that strengthen the dimension and a synthetic iconography that identifies each metric.

Fingerboards

Five screens complete MVP. I show them in the order that users conduct them.

Home - The 3 entrance gates

The hero opens with an explicit promise: "Visializes the data after your decisions." Below, three big cards run as entrance gates: Métrics, Framework and Roles. The fourth card, deliberately placed as a lateral feature, invites the user who still doesn't know how to start exploring the Roles Section, marked "In Development." That brand serves a dual purpose: it communicates honesty about the status of the product and at the same time valid interest without requiring that logic be complete.

Figure 10. Home. The three entrance gates to the system.

Meter catalogue - The exploration layer

The catalogue presents the thirteen MVP metrics in a grid with the common visual pattern: isometric illustration, HEART dimension by color and name + short description. The left side bar contains HEART filters as chips: Success, Happiness, Engagement, Retention and Adoption. The user can combine them without recharging the page, and a superior search engine allows shortcuts as soon as you know what you are looking for. The micro- interactions of filters and storage icons were specified in Figma with lover and activated states.

Figure 11. Catalogue of Métricas. HEART filters such as chips, search and cards with color-coded dimension.

HEART framework - The pedagogical phase

That's the theoretical anchor. The structure responds to a decision: each dimension is explained with the same template - what are you measuring, where to find it, why it matters, typical signs. The consistency of the template is what allows users to learn quickly: when they have already read the first dimension, they know what to expect from the remaining four. The Leader presents an illustration of the complete framework and an explanation that links HEART to the promise of the product: turn intuitions into evidence.

Figure 12. HEART framework. Reducible structure by dimension - Success, expanded at capture.

Roes - The implementation layer (currently under development)

The Roles Section is the visible Roadmap. Six cards have product cycle profiles - UX Designer, UI Designer, Growth Market, Data Analyst, Product Manager and Project Manager - each with a short description of what they do and what they measure. The detailed interplay (recommend metrics by role) remains as phase 2 of the product, but the page exists at MVP to validate interest and give rise to a second visit.

Figure 13. Roes. 6 profiles with navigable skeleton and recommendation logic remains Roadmap.

Meter Detail - The Depth Layer

The tab of each metric is the densiest piece of the product and the most time it took to design. The template always answers the same questions in the same order: Importance, Formula, Application, Cases of Use, Companies using them, Tip UX and Resources. That editorial consistency is strategic: the user learns his or her template once and then only has to learn his or her contents, and not his or her structure.

The left side bar works as a table of navigable contents, reducing scroll for users who come with a specific question. The "Cases of Use" Section occupies most of the page's vertically: it's the central Insight - see how Spotify, Google or Free Market apply the metric - that converts information into argument. At the end, a block of "related Métrics" invites users to continue to explore laterally, and not only in depth.

Figure 14. Ficha de detención de la metrics User Retention Rate. Diebar navigation + relative high and metric template.

CENTRAL HYMOTESIS OF THE PROTOTYPE

If the detail template is always the same, the cognitive cost of learning a new metric decreases with each visit. Editorial consistency is the true feature.

FASE 05 · TESTEAR - Validate with real users

The MVP's testing combined three methods: moderate usability tasks with five participants, a heuristic assessment using Nielsen's principles and the SUS (System Usability Scale) scale administered at the end of each session.

Test design

Five participants - three from the Carolina and two from the Mariana profiles - received three tasks in randomized order:

  • Task 1. Imagine you're working on redesigning an onboard and you need to measure if new users complete the first flow. Find the right metric and explain why you chose it.
  • Task 2. Your PM's asking you exactly what the HEART framework is. Find the information you need to answer them in less than two minutes.
  • Task 3. Your team's debating if User Retention Rate's the right metric. Find at least two cases of using companies using them to support your argument.

Qualitative findings

What worked

  • The repeatable template of the details sheet was mentioned by 4 out of 5 participants as "easy to read once you understand the pattern."
  • HEART filters as chips reduced metric location time versus free search, especially for midlevel participants.
  • The association color- dimension It was learned intuitively: after the second metric, the participants advanced the dimension by the color of the card.

What Failed

  • Strategy 3 - Cases of use. Two participants tried to search "cases" or "companies" at the main search engine before entering the details of the metric. Section's up, but it's undetectable from outside.
  • Sidebar from the tab. Three participants did not notice the internal navigation sisebar at the first visit. They did linear scroll instead of jumping between sections.
  • Section Roles. The brand "In Development" was interpreted by two participants as "this Section's broken." The intention (Validation of interest) was not transmitted.

RESULTS AND challenges

What the project taught

Datapiers close the academic cycle with a functional MVP, validated with actual users and tipped above the good usability threshold. More important than the metric, however, were the learnings that the process left about how to work research and translation to product.

Decisions I made and I'll make again

  • A single taxonomy. To cross the whole product with HEART eliminated conflicts of mental model. Each time I was tempted to add another classification (by industry, by product type), the simplicity of a single lens earned.
  • Reportable plate. The consistency of the detail sheet is what the testing best valued. What at first felt as editorial rigidity became the main driver of usability.
  • To say no to the multilingual. The product's competitive differentiation is about talking to LTAM in Spanish. Add english would have diluted the bet and doubled the editorial cost.
  • Show the "In Development. " Although the testing revealed that the brand confusing, showing the visible Roadmap was a right decision - just needs better copy.

Decisions to be re-evaluated

  • Local persistence rather than accounts. He covered MVP but the testing revealed a desire for synchronization between devices. The future product will likely require a minimum account system.
  • 13 metric in the catalogue. Sufficient to prove the hypothesis but some participants were expecting a more comprehensive catalogue. The balance between editorial depth and scope of the catalogue deserves more research.

PERSONAL REFLEXION

What I get

To design a product that teaches how to make decisions with data forced me to make my own decision with data. That symmetry between topic and method was, without me looking for it, the greatest lesson of the project.

Three considerations close the case:

Research

The initial six interviews have put me back at the first week. He had come to his project thinking that the user needed more information about metrics and research revealed that he needed less information, better cured. The difference between these two scenarios is the difference between a saturated and a useful product.

Priority

Deciding what remained outside of MVP was more difficult than deciding what was going on. The Impact vs. Efforts matrix did not give me answers and gave me a language to uphold my decisions with the team and especially with myself. Each dejected feature has a tractable justification.

About the design system

I learned that visual consistency isn't an aesthetic detail: it's the main vehicle for users to learn quickly. The repeatable template of the detail sheet, the five HEART colors and synthetic iconography did more for product usability than any feature that could have been added instead.

"The product isn't the sum of its features. It's the coherence of the mental model that invites the user to build."
