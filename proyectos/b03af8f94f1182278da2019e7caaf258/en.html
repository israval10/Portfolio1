Reebok Green

Globant Design Center Latam competition. Footwear analysis and gamified recycling app.

Automatically translated from the Spanish original.

Reebok Green was a proposal for the Globant Design Center Latam competition. The challenge was to help people choose sports shoes according to their activity, their preferences and their foot characteristics and give them a way to get back a pair used in store.

Initial alignment

At the kickoff we reviewed what the brief had to propose and the questions we needed to solve to design the experience. One of our hypotheses was that a form could collect the information necessary to recommend a pair. We also explore what value sustainability could bring to the purchase and return experience.

From Service Design, I put up two questions that affected range. The first was what information the physical store could provide that would be difficult to capture with a form. I propose to explore foot and wear analysis of shoes. The second was whether to customize means to recommend a catalogue model or to manufacture a pair on demand. Each option involved a different operation.

Definition of the problem

We came from the assumption that people used Reebok's channels mainly when they were considering or making a purchase. At the problem tree we have been exploring potential challenges, including an unconsistent interchannel experience, low visibility about product materials and an offer of discounts-focused incentives.

The hypothesis led us to work at two moments of our journey. When buying, the person should have been able to choose a pair with information relevant to his activity. After use, I should have been able to figure out where to get back and what I would get to do. The frequency with which people returned to Reebok's channels outside purchase was as an research question.

Prioritizing the proposal

We compare ideas by priority and estimated viability during the workshop. The customization of web and store, the tab with attributes and composition, the return of shoes and the system of points have been put into the quadrant of highest priority and viability. The augmented reality was also among the priority ideas with an estimated lower viability.

My proposals were to give the product a tab that made its attributes and materials visible and to use points to show progress to a benefit. Both ideas appear in the prototype, the purchase flow sheet and the confirmation of the recycling flow.

Personas and purchase journeys

At the second meeting we used two jobs archetypes to explain how the decision to buy changes with the person's and his channel's experience. We mapped his actions, his doubts and his design opportunities from searching for shoes to his return.

The exploratory survey had different reasons to buy from every channel. Among the answers to that question, there were those who preferred the store to have their shoes tested and those who preferred the web for saving time or easy finding offers. The tours of Adriana and Gabo explore these two ways of buying. In another question, 18 of 25 people (72%) said they had been interested in knowing their shoes. That information supported the fact that the composition was shown on the product sheet. Results of the FigJan survey

Adriana, specialist. He runs regularly and looks for slippers that suit his challenges and his style. At the workshop stage, she starts looking at the web and goes to the store to test models. That's where she expects specialist advice. He ends up buying a pair with features similar to those he was looking for and finds out about his pay back program with doubts about how it works.

Gabo, an amateur. He's about to start playing tennis and needs guidance about shoes and surfaces. On his web tour, I mapped how she researches, compares and filters models before she buys and how she checks the setting when she gets the pair. At the end of use, the tour raises another question: what to do with spent shoes and how recycling works.

The tours helped us realize two opportunities. During purchase, a recommendation had to explain why a model was matching with the activity and adjustment that a person was looking for. After use, the return program had to explain where to give up the pair and what benefit it would get. We use these scenarios to guide the design of purchase and return flows.

Priority for impact and effort

With the opportunities of the tours on the table, we estimate at the workshop how much value each proposal could bring and how much effort it would require. The matrix allowed us to record what ideas to first explore, what to develop after and what to deprioritize.

In "Do it now." Two ways remained to bring store experience closer to the digital channel. I propose to explore augmented reality to simulate some of the physical experience. The other proposal sought to move counselling through a virtual assistant or personalization questions. In Do it later. have been the scouting of his foot, his web and store customization, his product sheet, his return program and his rewards, among other initiatives.

The prioritization also leave ideas for after-purchase, such as order monitoring, care guides, collection of used shoes and an explanation of their environmental impact. In the prototype, the product sheet incorporated information about its attributes and composition and the return path showed the points received after giving up the pair.

Reebok Green Mission

To assist those who start a sport or practice it regularly to select shoes suitable for their activity and their type of foot and to give back their used pair when they have to replace them. Reebok Green propose custom recommendations and a refund process that explain what store to deliver the pair and what benefits are received.

The mission starts with two situations we have been exploring at the workshop. Adriana was looking for information on the web and needed store advice about her step. Gabo compared models, filters and height guides to start with tennis and, at the end of his use, had questions about what to do with his shoes. These tours helped to define what person should be able to do with the prototype: to understand why a model fits his activity and to know what steps to follow to get back a pair that appears in "My slippers."

Map of service actors and roles

From Service Design, I mapped with the team who interacted from the design of a slipper to his return. The exercise connected product equipment, factory and warehouse with purchase and delivery channels. We also identify those who influence the choice, as training partners, online communities and content creators.

The map helped us understand what co-ordination was behind a recommendation. At store, counselling depends on the equipment knowing the characteristics of shoes. The information sheet, sizes and availability depend on the work among those who manage the catalogue and inventory.

To get back, the prototype shows how the client chooses a store and sees the points associated with delivery. From that moment on, the map sets out the tasks of receiving, registering and grading used shoes and their potential use as raw material in manufacturing. That's how the units that have to be solved to get service to operation are visible.

Service system

After we identify roles, we place Reebok Green at the centre and organize service elements as they come close with the client. The first layer included the physical store, app and web, electronic commerce and home delivery. Around we place the physical services, technologies and actors that support these contact points.

Gabo's tour helps to read the map. To get an order is his contact point. The logistics company, distribution, vehicles and delivery personnel are involved for this. At the back, the prototype allows you to choose a store. The workshop identified what would be necessary from the delivery of the pair: a recycling centre, a reception equipment and resources to process materials.

Jobs to Be Do

From the Adriana and Gabo scenarios, we put forward what each person wanted to achieve in a concrete situation. For each case we record the context that triggers the search, the assistance that you expect to get and the result that you want to achieve.

At the Adriana, choosing well means understanding if the model conforms to its step and serves to run with a respectful advice that also takes its style into account. At GaboI did have two needs. At the start of a sport, she wants information that will allow her to choose the right shoes. When you stop using a pair, you want a simple choice to recycle and help reduce waste.

The shared needs resulted in three parts of the prototype. The form includes activity and preferences to guide the recommendation. The tab allows to review the model's characteristics and composition. The recycling flow indicates where to deliver the used pair. That's how people were looking for a model with specific characteristics.

Review of priority and viability

After we mapped the service actors, we re-revised the proposals considering the role of the store, the digital channel and the recycling centre.

The customization, return and points system remained high priority and more viability. To show the shoes's life cycle was incorporated into that quadrant. The gamified community remained high priority and moved to the least viability quadrant.

That review left explicit two scope for rewards. On the one hand, a system of points to show progress to a benefit. On the other, a gamified community for customers to share experiences. Digital advice and augmented reality remained among the priority ideas of less viability.

From objectives to functionalities

We organize proposals in seven areas of service to move from general objectives to concrete functions. The board collects ideas for customization, search and information, delivery, use and care, recycling and rewards, collection and community.

Three ideas can be followed up to the prototype:

  • Personalization. The flow includes activity and preferences on a form, passing through foot analysis and presenting a catalogue model recommendation.
  • Information to choose from. The product sheet brings together the attributes and composition of the shoes so that the person can review the suggested model.
  • Returns and rewards. The flow shows the condition of the used pair, helps select a store to deliver and presents the points associated with the return.

The impact and effort matrix put the scouting, tab and return with points at "Do It Later." On the feature board, the areas of customization and recycling and rewards had four stars each. The augmented reality had been put into "Do It Now" and the review was among the less viability ideas. The final criterion leading to the inclusion of scanning in the prototype was undocumented.

The proposals for monitoring the order, care guides, collection and community were documented as opportunities to extend the service.

Flow of task

After we have ordered the functionalities, we have drawn five tours to figure out what Adriana and Gabo would do on the web and how to get the store involved. At the back sketch, Adriana looks at the online program, gives the person's pair and puts a discount on his next purchase. Gabo, for his part, finds jobs to gain points and goes through with the process and confirms an exchange.

To buy, we scout two tours. At one, Adriana complete a form, select a suggestion and load a photo of his foot. On the other, apply filters, answer questions and save two models to favorites before registration. A fifth tour allows you to return to the account to check the life cycle of the returned pair, points and discounts.

In-store returns

On the "11.03 - Flow" board, the return starts with "My slippers," inside the profile. The person opens the tab of a bought pair that already appears on his profile, chooses to recite and proceeds to his or her status. The tour puts forward two ways to provide an image for diagnosis: using a scanner or uploading a photo.

Exchange of points

At the proposed flow, the person login or register to gain access to Rewards, where his or her balance and catalogue are consulted. When selecting a reward, the flow checks if you have enough points. If you reach the required balance, move to confirmation and collection. If you have a few points missing, see options to gain more.

Test with eight participants

We tested the prototype with eight people to watch how they found the custom purchase path and what they felt about the form's questions. The stage of the script was to look for custom hiking shoes. Test script at FigJam

Before the task, we asked about online purchase habits, physical activity and using shoes. During the tour, we asked them to describe out loud what they saw, what they would do next and what they hoped would have happened if they stopped. At close, we were picking up his prints.

The sessions have three points from the course.

  • Find options for sport. One participant responded to the search warrant after a technical incident. The note also indicates that options needed to be deployed at sports. I noted that the tabs or filters were family checks that people hoped to find.
  • Value the suggested model. One person thought about going back after seeing that the first choice didn't suit. The notes mention damping, foot setting, price and offers among the criteria used to evaluate options.
  • Use form as necessary. Questions brought interactivity and some were difficult to answer. I also noted that a person who already knows what his shoes require should be able to continue without complete his form.

After testing, we propose to repropose difficult questions, to facilitate the form's output and to offer more alternatives as soon as the first model suggested did not fit.

Creation of the Design System

The 44-view audit of the prototype found that the store, the recommender and the recycling used different patterns to navigate, advance a form and load photos. During redesign, a local library was established at Figma to share these patterns among the three tours. Design System Library in Figma

Visual foundations

The system linked the base palette with variables that define the use of each color at the interface, such as main action, focus or status messages. Lexend was also defined for the interface, Anton for campaign holders and rules of space, grid, edges and elevation for mobile, tablet and desktop.

Components and states

The buttons, navigation, cards, step indicator and image uploader were built as reusable components with Auto Layout. Each component incorporated the states it needed. For example, foot analysis and pair diagnosis share the load pattern with their own instructions for each photo type, a processing status and an action to retry if there's an error. Loading component in Figma

Application to prototype

Five pilot screens were reviewed at Figma at 320, 360, 412, 768 and 1280 px to check how controls and cards were re-distributed by changing their width. In final flows, the shared header, indicator of achievement and load states show how the library was applied to the screens.
